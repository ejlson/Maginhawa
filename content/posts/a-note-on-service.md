My parents opened Bintang on Kentish Town Road in 1987 with a dining room
that sat under thirty people. There was no service manual. There was my
mother, who could tell from the door whether a table had come to celebrate
something or to survive the week, and who cooked differently depending on
the answer.

## What we mean by service

Everything since — the ice cream parlour, the ramen counter, the bistro two
minutes' walk from where we started — has been an attempt to write that
down without killing it. It resists being written down. What we have
instead is a short list of things that are true in every room we run.

- A guest should never have to ask twice for anything.
- The person who takes the order is the person who is responsible for it.
- If a dish leaves the pass late, it leaves the pass free.
- Nobody eats badly on their birthday because we were busy.

None of that is hospitality theatre. It is closer to housekeeping: the
unglamorous, repeatable things that let a room look after people on a
Tuesday in February as well as it does on a Saturday in December.

## Different rooms, different stories

The kitchens have very little in common on paper. Bintang cooks Filipino
with a fusion accent; Guanabana cooks Caribbean; Belly reads Filipino
flavour through a French bistro lens. A guest moving between them should
notice how different they are. They should not be able to tell the
difference in how they are treated.

> One standard, held in eight rooms, is harder than eight standards held
> once each.

That is the whole ambition, and it is the reason this journal exists. Most
of what gets written about restaurants is written from the dining room
looking in. This is the view from the pass — openings, decisions, the
occasional thing we got wrong and fixed.

We will not post often. When we do, it will be because something actually
happened.
